---
title: Tenants and workspaces
description: How an account is divided, and how to give someone only one part of it.
---

Two boundaries, doing two different jobs. Getting them the right way round is
what makes the rest of access control make sense.

| | Answers | Crosses? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Account** | Whose data is this? | Never. Nothing crosses between accounts |
| **Workspace** | Which part of the organisation is this? | Only if you grant it |

You get one account. Inside it you create as many workspaces as you need.

## What a workspace is

A workspace groups offices, automations, conversations and data so a dispatch
team and a finance team can work in the same account without seeing each other's
work.

It is also **the resource almost every permission names**. That is the part
worth internalising: when you grant someone a role, you are nearly always
granting it on a workspace. So the workspace is not just an organisational
convenience. It is the unit of access.

<Callout>
  **The record itself is tiny.** A workspace is a name and a timezone.
  Everything you think of as living "in" it is a relationship held on the thing
  itself. This is why deleting a workspace does not delete its contents.
</Callout>

## Giving each customer their own

If you serve clients rather than internal teams, the same structure does
something more interesting: **one workspace per customer**, all inside your
single account.

```
  your account
    ├── workspace: Northside Dental     ← their staff, only this
    ├── workspace: Riverside Legal      ← their staff, only this
    └── workspace: Harbour Fitness      ← their staff, only this
         ▲
         └── your team, across all of them
```

Each customer gets an isolated place to work. Your team works across all of
them. Nobody on one side sees the other's.

This is a common shape for anyone reselling a service, and it works here because
of how permissions resolve rather than because of a separate feature you have to
switch on.

### How the isolation actually happens

There is no "restrict to one workspace" setting. The containment is a
consequence of two ordinary rules.

<Steps>

<Step>
**Grant the customer's people a role on their workspace, and nowhere else.**
An assignment names a boundary. A role assigned at `workspace/W1` grants
nothing at `workspace/W2`, because W2 is not inside W1.
</Step>

<Step>
**Do not give them anything at `console`.**
The console scope is a sibling root, not a parent, so a workspace grant never
reaches it. But the reverse matters more here: console grants are the ones that
reach across an account, and creating a workspace is a console action. Someone
without console access cannot make themselves a second workspace.
</Step>

</Steps>

The result shows up immediately in the API.
[List workspaces](/docs/api-reference/workspaces/workspaces/listWorkspaces) has
no gate of its own. It checks `workspace:read` against each workspace
separately and returns only the ones that pass, so a person scoped to one
workspace gets exactly one entry and no indication the others exist.

<Callout type="warn">
  **Console access is the thing to withhold.** Almost everything that reaches
  across an account is scoped there: creating workspaces, managing members,
  editing roles and policies, billing. A customer's staff should hold no console
  grant at all, and your own team should hold it deliberately rather than by
  default.
</Callout>

### Some things are account-wide on purpose

Not everything belongs to a workspace. A few resources exist once for the whole
account, such as integrations you publish for reuse and account-level settings.
They are managed at console scope, which is the other reason to be careful about
who holds it.

When you need to be certain what a particular person can reach, check it rather
than reasoning it out. The console can evaluate a specific action against a
specific resource for a specific user, which is faster than deriving it from
their assignments.

## Capping what each one can spend

A workspace can carry its own spend limit. When the cap is reached, work in
**that** workspace is refused until the window resets, and every other workspace
carries on unaffected.

That makes per-customer ceilings straightforward: give each workspace a monthly
budget and one customer's heavy month cannot consume another's headroom.

```bash
curl -X POST "$HOS_API/workspaces/ws_a1b2c3/budgets" \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $HOS_API_KEY" \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{
    "name": "Monthly model spend",
    "metric": ["platform", "byok"],
    "limitUsd": 250,
    "window": "monthly"
  }'
```

<Callout type="warn">
  **A budget caps model spend, not everything.** The cost of running machines is
  not counted and cannot be limited this way, so a budget is a ceiling on one
  kind of spend rather than a total. [Spending](/docs/spend) covers what each
  meter measures.
</Callout>

A budget can also cap what employees spend on **your own model account**, which
is money we never bill you for. That is something a credit balance cannot do,
and it is usually the real answer to keeping model costs contained. See
[Spending](/docs/spend).

Budgets reset on the workspace's **own** calendar, using its timezone. Two
workspaces in different regions roll over at different moments, which is usually
what you want when the customers are in different places. Set it with
[Set the timezone](/docs/api-reference/workspaces/workspaces/updateWorkspaceTimeConfig).

## Deleting one

<Callout type="warn">
  **Deleting a workspace does not delete what is inside it.** Offices,
  automations and data are not removed, they stop being reachable, and anything
  that was running keeps running. Empty a workspace before you delete it.
</Callout>

Role assignments pointing at the deleted workspace also remain. They resolve to
nothing, and clearing them keeps the account's access model honest.
