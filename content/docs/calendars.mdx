title: Calendars and booking
description: One calendar over several accounts, shared with people and employees alike.
---

A calendar here is not a copy of your Google or Outlook calendar. It is a
container you create, point at one or more real accounts, and then share.

Reads and writes pass straight through to those accounts. Nothing is duplicated,
so nothing drifts, and deleting anything here never deletes anything there.

## One view, several accounts

Bind more than one account and they merge into a single view. Each keeps its own
colour, and any of them can be muted without unbinding it.

```
   Sales calendar
     ├─ google      hello@acme.com        ● shown
     ├─ outlook     bookings@acme.com     ● shown
     └─ google      personal@…            ○ muted
```

An office's scheduled work can appear alongside them, so what an AI employee is
booked to do sits beside what people are booked to do. That view is read-only:
schedules are not created here.

## Membership is what grants access

This is the part that surprises people, and it is worth getting right before you
debug a refusal.

A calendar belongs to a workspace, but **the workspace is not what decides who
may use it.** The member list is.

| To | You need |
| --- | --- |
| Read or write events and tasks | To be a member. Holding the manage permission is not enough |
| Read the calendar, its members, its accounts | Membership **or** the manage permission |
| Rename it, bind accounts, add members, delete it | The manage permission. Membership not required |

A member from **any** workspace passes. So sharing a calendar with another team
means adding them to its member list, not moving anything.

The split is deliberate: **governing a calendar and using one are different
jobs.** Someone can administer a calendar they cannot read the appointments in.

<Callout type="warn">
  **A refusal has two possible causes and they look identical.** Either the
  permission is missing, or the permission is there and the caller is not a
  member. Check the member list before assuming a role is wrong.
</Callout>

## An AI employee is a member like anyone else

An office joins the member list as an ordinary row, and then calls the same
endpoints a person calls, through the same checks.

There is no agent path and no elevated access. You give an employee a calendar
the same way you give a colleague one, and you take it away the same way.

This is the cleanest example of the idea on the platform: the thing that decides
whether an AI employee can see Thursday afternoon is a row in a list, which you
can look at.

## Letting other people book

A booking link is a page someone outside your account opens to pick a slot. What
they book becomes an event on your calendar and can start an
[automation](/docs/workflows).

You can hand out a reusable page, or mint a one-time link for a specific person.
A one-time link is a snapshot: it copies the settings as they are when you create
it, so later changes do not reach links already sent.

<Callout type="warn">
  **A link's address must be unique across the whole platform**, not just your
  account. A name someone else has taken is refused even though it is not taken
  by you. Prefer something specific to something generic.
</Callout>

### Deciding when people can book

Availability comes from five things, and knowing which one to reach for saves a
lot of guessing:

| To block out | Use |
| --- | --- |
| A recurring day, or part of one | Working hours. A weekday you leave out has no availability at all |
| One specific date | A date override, set to nothing |
| Time you are genuinely busy | Nothing. Real events on the bound accounts already remove those slots |
| Breathing room around each booking | A buffer before, after, or both |
| Volume | A cap per day, or per slot |

A date override **replaces** that day's hours rather than adding to them, which
is what makes an empty one mean closed.

The part you do not configure is the useful part: **anything already in your
calendar removes the slots it covers.** Blocking Thursday afternoon is a matter
of putting something in your own calendar.

<Callout type="warn">
  **A day with a cap cannot be rescheduled within itself.** A booking being moved
  still counts toward the day it is leaving, so once the cap is reached, moving
  it to a later slot the same day is refused. Moving it to another day works.
</Callout>

## Writes go to a real account

Creating an event writes it into the Google or Outlook account you name, and it
appears here because that account is bound.

Two consequences worth planning for:

- **You have to say where.** Every write names the account and the calendar
  inside it. There is no implicit destination.
- **Deleting a calendar deletes nothing at the provider.** You lose the
  container, its members and its bindings. The appointments stay where they were
  written.

<Callout>
  **A broken connection stays broken.** If an account's authorisation fails,
  syncing will not repair it. Rebind the account.
</Callout>
