---
title: Offices and employees
description: What an office is, why every employee gets one, and what lives inside it.
---

An **AI employee** is the thing you hire. An **office** is the machine it works
on. One employee, one office, always.

That sounds like a detail. It is the design decision the rest of the platform
rests on, so it is worth understanding before you build anything.

## Why an employee gets a whole machine

Most AI products give you a conversation. You send text, you get text back, and
nothing persists. That works until the employee has to do something real.

An office is a private computer. It has its own filesystem, its own terminal, and
its own state that survives between turns. An employee working in one can clone a
repository on Monday and still have it on Thursday. It can install what it needs.
It can leave itself notes.

It also has its own identity. Not yours, not a shared one. The office
authenticates as itself, holds its own permissions, and everything it does is
attributable to it.

<Callout>
  The isolation is the point. One employee cannot read another's files, reach
  another's credentials, or use another's tools. That is not a policy, it is a
  boundary: two offices are two machines.
</Callout>

## What is attached to an office

An office starts empty. You attach what the employee needs.

| Attached | What it gives the employee |
| --- | --- |
| **Integrations** | Software it can call, and the credentials to call it |
| **Skills** | Things it knows how to do |
| **Protocols** | Rules it must always follow |
| **Namespaces** | Data it can read and write |
| **Apps and websites** | Software and sites it may reach |
| **Browsers** | A real Chromium for systems with no API |
| **Phone numbers** | Send and receive SMS, and answer calls |
| **Email addresses** | Send and receive mail as a real address |
| **Secrets** | Its own vault, injected when it boots |

Everything on that list is scoped to the office. Attaching a namespace to one
office does not give any other office access to it.

### They are not all attached the same way

Three different mechanisms, and knowing which is which saves a lot of searching.

| Mechanism | Assets | Where you do it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Allocated to the office** | Namespaces, apps, websites | The office's own endpoints |
| **Membership on the asset** | Browsers, email addresses | On the browser or the address, adding the office as a member |
| **Console only** | Phone numbers | The console. Buying one spends money, so a person does it. |

The middle row is the one people look for in the wrong place. There is no
"allocate a browser" endpoint on an office. You
[grant the office access on the browser](/docs/api-reference/browsers/members/addBrowserMember),
and the office's browser list is a view of grants made there.

Once any of these is attached, the employee uses it with its own key like any
other capability. **Acquiring is sometimes a human action. Using never is.**

See [Phone numbers and email](/docs/api-reference/phone-and-email) for both
planes of those two.

## Creating one provisions real infrastructure

An office is not a database row. Creating one starts a machine, a data plane
entry, a container project and service, and a service account credential.

<Callout type="warn">
  **The office existing does not mean the office is ready.** The infrastructure
  steps are deliberately non-blocking, so a failure logs a warning rather than
  failing your request. After creating an office, check its cloud config and its
  API keys before assuming it can run.
</Callout>

That design keeps a partial failure recoverable instead of leaving you with a
half created office to clean up. Both pieces have a repair path.

See [Create an office](/docs/api-reference/offices/offices/createOffice).

## You choose how long an office stays awake

An office can scale to zero when idle, or stay running as long as you want. That
is a setting, not a fixed behaviour.

`idleTimeoutMinutes` decides it. A short timeout means the machine sleeps
quickly, costs nothing at rest, and pays for that with a cold start on the next
job. A long one keeps it warm and responsive, and it is awake, and billed, the
whole time.

Neither is the right answer in general. An employee that answers customers during
business hours wants to be warm. One that runs a nightly reconciliation does not
need to exist for the other twenty three hours.

Sleeping costs you nothing and loses you nothing: state persists on the
filesystem, so an office that wakes on Thursday still has what it did on Monday.

## You can resize the machine

`instanceType` selects the machine tier. An employee doing light triage and one
compiling code do not need the same computer, and you are not locked into the
size you started with.

**The available tiers depend on the service type**, so read the current
configuration to see what you can choose rather than assuming a list. Both
settings live on the same endpoint:

apart from the right to operate the employee.

<Callout>
  **This is Humanik Cloud underneath.** An office exists there as a service, and

  you should usually stay on the office route.
</Callout>

## Employees are identities, not sessions

Because an office authenticates as itself, an AI employee is a first class
identity in the permission system. It holds role assignments in the same form a
person does, evaluated by the same engine.

An employee can therefore be granted exactly one action on exactly one resource,
or a workspace wide role, or anything between. It is the same model you use for
your team.

There is one asymmetry, and it favours safety: an employee can never hold the
owner bypass that a tenant owner does. Machines are governed more strictly than
people, not less.

See [How access control works](/docs/access-control).
