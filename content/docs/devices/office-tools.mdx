---
title: Tools your machine does not hold
description: Turning an office into an execution environment for the credentialed half of the work.
---

An employee running on your machine can do two kinds of thing in one breath: work
on your computer, and work that needs a company credential your computer has no
business holding.

It does the first here and the second in its office, and it does not experience
them as different. That split is the point of this page, and it is what makes it
reasonable to run real work on a laptop.

The split exists because a flavor B machine runs a **capable agent** rather than
serving pure inference. It has tools of its own, on your computer, so a turn
spans two places at once. This page assumes that flavor throughout.

<ToolSplit />

## The office is the place credentials already are

An office is a machine of its own with its own vault. Everything you connect to
it, along with everything it ships with, is available to the employee as tools
with names.

**Your machine never learns any of it.** No key is written to it, sent to it, or
readable from it. When the employee needs one, the call is made in the office and
only the answer comes back.

That holds even for a machine you trust completely, and it matters more the less
you own the machine: a contractor's laptop can run work against your billing
system without ever being able to make that call on its own.

## What the office brings to the turn

Two kinds of tool, and they behave the same way from the employee's side.

### The ones that ship

Every office starts with a working set, without you configuring anything: its
[contacts and conversations](/docs/crm), its
[calendars and booking links](/docs/calendars), its
[files and namespaces](/docs/namespaces), the
[browsers](/docs/api-reference/browsers) it has been given, its own schedules,
image generation, and the ability to read its own
[skills](/docs/skills-and-protocols) and integrations.

These are compiled into the office. There is nothing to enable and nothing that
can drift.

### The ones you connect

Everything else comes from an [integration](/docs/integrations). Each endpoint
you declare and enable becomes a tool the employee can call by name, with its own
parameter schema, and the credential stays where you put it. See
[Integration skills](/docs/integrations/skills) for how the employee is taught to
use one, and [Creating an integration](/docs/integrations/creating) for declaring
the endpoints.

<Callout>
  **An integration cannot take over a tool that ships.** Names are resolved
  against the built in set first, and only a miss falls through to your
  integrations, so an integration reusing a shipped name is ignored rather than
  shadowing it. Two integrations declaring the same name keep the first and warn
  about the second.
</Callout>

## How the call actually crosses

What happens next depends on which flavor of machine you are running, and the
difference is worth knowing because it changes what a turn feels like.

<Tabs items={['Flavor B, an agent', 'Flavor A, a model']}>

<Tab value="Flavor B, an agent">

The agent on your machine calls the tool and waits. The call travels up the same
connection your machine already holds, HumanikOS routes it to that office's
machine, the office runs it with its own credentials, and the result comes back
down. The agent picks up in the same turn, exactly where it stopped.

This is what makes a single instruction like "reconcile this local file against
last month's payouts" work: the reading and the writing happen on your disk, the
payout lookup happens in the office, and there is no round trip through a person
in between.

**One tool call is capped at about two minutes.** A tool that takes longer fails
with a readable error rather than leaving the agent blocked for the rest of the
turn.

**Pictures come back too.** A tool that returns a screenshot or a rendered image
sends it alongside its text rather than encoded inside it, so the model gets both
the picture and the context for it.

</Tab>

<Tab value="Flavor A, a model">

A local model is a model, so it ends its turn by saying which tool it wants and
stopping. The office runs the tool and starts the next turn with the result
already in hand.

The tool still runs in the office, with the office's credentials, and your
machine still never sees them.

<Callout>
  **On a flavor A machine there is no local half.** The machine has no tools, no
  working directory, and no connectors, because a model has nothing to apply them
  to. Every tool in the turn is an office tool. The page you are reading still
  applies, it is just that the split it describes has only one side.

  Everything about acting on your own computer needs
  [flavor B](/docs/devices#three-kinds-of-device).
</Callout>

</Tab>

</Tabs>

<Callout type="warn">
  **The third flavor does not do this at all.** An OpenClaw harness device brings
  its own tools and calls the platform itself, so no office tool is offered to it.
  It is also not available yet.
</Callout>

## A worked example

Say you have a Stripe integration on the office and the employee is running on
your laptop:

> *"Reconcile yesterday's payouts against the ledger in `~/finance/2026-08.csv`
> and write me a summary."*

<Steps>

<Step>
**It reads `~/finance/2026-08.csv`** with `Read`, on your machine. That file never
leaves it, and no copy is uploaded.
</Step>

<Step>
**It calls `stripe_list_payouts`.** That call does not run here. It goes up to the
office, which holds the Stripe key, makes the request, and sends back only the
response.
</Step>

<Step>
**It compares the two** and writes the summary to a path you allowed.
</Step>

</Steps>

Steps 1 and 3 happen on your hardware under your ceiling. Step 2 happens in the
office under the office's permissions. The employee does not distinguish between
them, because from where it sits they are all just tools.

**Local files stay local.** The office never sees `~/finance/2026-08.csv`. It
sees a request for a list of payouts, and answers it.

## The credential never enters the conversation

This is worth stating on its own, because the obvious implementation gets it
wrong.

The employee supplies arguments. The office attaches the credential to the
outgoing request. At no point is a key placed in the employee's context, which
means it is not in the transcript, not in any log that captures one, and not in
anything that travels to your machine.

The office reads the value from its vault when the call is made, not from
anything the model said, and holds it briefly rather than keeping it around.

**Arguments the endpoint did not declare are dropped rather than forwarded.** If
a model invents a parameter, it does not reach the third party. An unfilled
placeholder in a path is refused rather than sent literally.

## Setting it up

<Steps>

<Step>
**Connect what the office needs.** Create the [integrations](/docs/integrations)
on the office, declare each endpoint you want callable with every parameter given
a location, and point them at a secret group. A tenant vault group is the right
home for anything shared across employees.
</Step>

<Step>
**Restart the office.** The callable set is built when the office starts.
Declaring an endpoint on a running office does not add the tool until it comes
back up.
</Step>

<Step>
**Enrol your machine** and choose flavor B, which is the one that behaves like an
agent. [Connect your own machine](/docs/devices) covers the whole install, and it
is the point where you decide the ceiling.
</Step>

<Step>
**Set the ceiling for the local half.** Only the tools you name are permitted on
your machine, and the default is read only. This governs what happens on your
computer and has no effect on what the office may do.
</Step>

<Step>
**Allocate the machine to the workspace**, then point the office at it. Both
steps, and the request that does the second one, are on
[Bring your own compute](/docs/models/your-own-compute).
</Step>

<Step>
**Restart the office again** so it picks up the machine, then check the board:
the employee's node changes shape when it is running on your hardware. See
[how it looks](/docs/models/your-own-compute#how-it-looks-on-the-board).
</Step>

</Steps>

Then confirm the machine side is sound without spending anything:

```bash
humanikd verify     # everything this flavor needs
humanikd status     # what this machine is advertising right now
```

## The rules that keep the two halves apart

**Your ceiling governs your machine, and only your machine.** Widening it does
not widen what the office may do, and narrowing it does not restrict the office.
The ceiling exists to protect your computer, not to bound the employee.

**The office's permissions govern the office, and only the office.** An
integration reaching an API is doing so with what that office was granted. It has
no route back onto your machine.

**Nothing upstream can name your tools.** The job never carries a tool list, a
working directory, or a permission mode. Your machine intersects what is asked
with what you allowed, and never widens.

**The employee is told which is which.** It is briefed explicitly on the two
trust domains rather than left to work out from a name whether a tool runs on
your disk or in the office.

## When a tool is not there

Almost always the integration, not the device.

<Steps>

<Step>
**Check the endpoint is declared and enabled**, and that every parameter says
where it belongs. A parameter with no location is dropped at call time, which
looks like a broken tool rather than a missing field.
</Step>

<Step>
**Check the integration has a base address and a credential.** One with neither
registers no tools at all. That is a valid thing to have, and the employee is
told plainly that it has no callable endpoints rather than being invited to
invent names.
</Step>

<Step>
**Restart the office.** The registry is built at boot, so a declaration added
since the last start is not loaded.
</Step>

</Steps>

<Callout type="warn">
  **Do not trust a message telling you the office needs redeploying.** When a
  single tool is missing, the employee can report it as an out of date office.
  One tool failing is not evidence of that, and a redeploy will not fix a
  declaration problem. Work through the three steps above first.
</Callout>
