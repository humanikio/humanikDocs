title: Connect your own machine

A **device** is a computer you enrol into a workspace so it can do work for you.
The daemon that runs on it is called `humanikd`. It holds one outbound connection
to HumanikOS, waits for jobs, and runs them locally.

Nothing about that connection lets HumanikOS reach into the machine. The daemon
dials out; there is no inbound port, and no job may widen what it is allowed to
do.

## Three kinds of device

A machine serves exactly one of these. The shape of its config file is the
declaration, so the two can never disagree.

| Flavor | What it serves | Needs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A**, local model | Completions from Ollama or a compatible server | Ollama running |
| **B**, Claude Code agent | An agent turn; its output *is* the reply | Claude Code, signed in |
| **C**, OpenClaw harness | A full agent framework, sandboxed | Docker |

Flavor B is the common one, and the rest of this section assumes it.

<Callout>
  **A machine can also do the thinking.** Flavors A and B can serve models to an
  office or to Nova, so work runs on your hardware instead of a vendor's. See
  [Bring your own compute](/docs/models/your-own-compute) for allocating one and
  for how a machine's tools and an office's tools coexist in a single turn.
</Callout>

## The rule that makes this safe

**The device declares a ceiling. A job may request a subset of it, and never
more.**

That single sentence is the whole security model. The ceiling lives in a config
file on your machine, written by you. HumanikOS never sends a tool list, a working
directory, or a permission mode. It sends the conversation and what it wants
done. If the control plane were compromised tomorrow, it still could not widen
what any enrolled machine is permitted to do.

The default ceiling is read-only:

```yaml
backend:
  claude_agent:
    enabled: true
    permission_mode: dontAsk
    allowed_tools: [Read, Grep, Glob]
```

Widening it is a deliberate, local decision. `Write`, `Edit` and `Bash` are all
available; none of them is on unless you put it there.

## Where an agent can reach

Four surfaces, and they are not equivalent. Knowing which is which is the
difference between a device that does what you meant and one that does more.

| Surface | Runs where | Configured by | In the ceiling |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Built-in tools**: `Read`, `Bash`, `Write` | This machine, in a derived workspace directory | You | Yes |
| **Inherited [MCP](https://modelcontextprotocol.io)**: your Gmail, Linear, Figma | Wherever that server points | You, on this machine | Yes |
| **Upstream tools**: Nova's, an office's | On HumanikOS, under the caller's own permissions | The platform, per job | No, by design |
| **[Browser](/docs/devices/browser)**: your real Chrome | In your browser, as you | You, with a separate key | Yes, **plus** its own key |

The first two are bounded by a workspace directory the daemon derives and the
server cannot name. **Upstream tools** are not gated by your ceiling because they
never touch your machine. They run on HumanikOS under the permissions of whoever
called them, and your device holds no credentials for them.

That includes the [integrations](/docs/integrations) you connect to an office. Their
endpoints become tools the employee calls through its office, so an employee running on your
machine reaches the same software as one running in the cloud. Nothing is installed here, and
no key is copied to this machine.

**The browser is the one that behaves differently**, and it has
[its own page](/docs/devices/browser) for that reason.

## Using your office's integrations from here

The conceptual split, with a diagram and a side by side comparison, is on
[Your own compute](/docs/models/your-own-compute#one-turn-two-sets-of-tools). This section is
the practical half: what your **integrations** do when the employee runs on your machine.

Short answer, they work unchanged. An employee running on your machine is not a cut down
version of one running in the cloud. It does the local work locally and the credentialed work
in the office, inside a single turn, without you wiring anything together.

Say you have a Stripe integration on the office and the employee is running on your laptop:

> *"Reconcile yesterday's payouts against the ledger in `~/finance/2026-08.csv` and write me
> a summary."*

1. It reads `~/finance/2026-08.csv` with `Read`. That file never leaves your machine, and no
   copy of it is uploaded.
2. It calls `stripe_list_payouts`. That call does not run here. It goes up to the office,
   which holds the Stripe key, makes the request, and sends back only the response.
3. It compares the two and writes the summary to a path you allowed.

Steps 1 and 3 happen on your hardware under your ceiling. Step 2 happens on HumanikOS under
the office's permissions. The employee does not distinguish between them, because from where
it sits they are all just tools.

### What this means in practice

**Your integrations work here, unchanged.** Anything you connect to the office is available
to an employee running on your machine. There is no separate device side integration to
configure, and nothing to keep in sync.

**Credentials stay in the office.** Your Stripe key, your CRM token and your internal API
credentials are never written to this machine, never sent to it, and are not readable from it.
The device receives responses, not keys. This holds even for a device you fully trust, which
matters more when the device is a colleague's laptop than when it is your own.

**Local files stay local.** The office never sees `~/finance/2026-08.csv`. It sees a request
for a list of payouts, and answers it. What the employee does with the answer happens here.

**Your ceiling still governs the local half.** Widening a device's tools does not widen what
its office tools can do, and an office integration cannot reach back onto your machine.

### When an integration tool is not there

If the employee reports that a tool does not exist, the integration is the place to look, not
the device. Check that the endpoint is declared and enabled on the integration, then restart
the office so it picks up the change. Nothing about the device needs to change.

## Getting started

```bash
curl -fsSL https://raw.githubusercontent.com/humanikio/humanikd-dist/main/install.sh | sh
humanikd setup
```

`humanikd setup` walks the whole thing: platform check, flavor choice, backend
prerequisites, enrolment, config, and auto-start. It never installs anything on
your behalf. It prints the command and waits.

Then, any time:

```bash
humanikd verify     # checks everything this flavor needs, spends no model quota
humanikd status     # what this device is advertising right now
```

## Two things worth knowing early

**Config is read once at startup.** There is no hot reload. After editing the file:

```bash
humanikd service restart
```

**Flavor B runs as *you*.** It installs a per-user service, not a system one,
because it has to read the `claude` login from your own keychain. That also means
the agent inherits your MCP connectors, which is usually the point, and
occasionally a surprise.
