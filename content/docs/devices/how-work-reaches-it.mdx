title: How work reaches your machine
description: The connection, the queue, and the trip a job makes from the workspace to your hardware and back.

Your machine sits behind a router that blocks incoming connections. It has no
fixed address on the internet, and it holds no password we issued. We still have
to send it work.

Everything on this page follows from that. It is worth understanding once,
because it explains the behaviour you will actually notice: why a closed laptop
is not an error, why a machine appears within seconds of waking, and why nothing
we run can reach into your computer.

## The direction is inverted

Nothing on HumanikOS connects to your machine, because nothing can. Instead the
daemon on your machine reaches out to one address and holds that connection open
for as long as it is switched on, the way a chat client stays logged in.

Work then travels down the connection your own machine opened.

<Steps>

<Step>
**Your machine dials out.** One outbound connection to one address, set in your
config file.
</Step>

<Step>
**The gateway asks it to prove who it is.** It sends a fresh random challenge and
gives the daemon ten seconds to answer.
</Step>

<Step>
**The daemon signs the challenge** with the private key it generated locally at
enrolment and has never sent anywhere.
</Step>

<Step>
**The gateway checks the signature** against the public half, and checks that the
machine has not been revoked. Both must pass, and a failure of either returns the
same message.
</Step>

<Step>
**The machine is now reachable**, and it says what it is serving: its flavor, and
the models it can run.
</Step>

</Steps>

<Callout>
  **The challenge is new every time.** A signature captured from one connection
  is worthless on the next, and nothing anywhere stores a signature to compare
  against. The gateway holds no secret of yours: it verifies against a public
  key, which is public by definition.
</Callout>

## Why the queue is addressed to a gateway instance

This is the fact that explains most of the rest of the behaviour.

Several gateway instances may be running, and your machine's connection lives in
the memory of exactly one of them. A connection cannot be shared or moved. So the
platform keeps a pointer, `device:{deviceId}` naming the instance that currently
holds you, and the job is queued to that instance rather than to your machine.

The pointer is rewritten every 25 seconds by the instance holding the connection,
and expires after 90 if it stops.

```
   device:{deviceId}  ->  instanceId        the pointer, 90s
   relay:inbox:{instanceId}                 that instance's queue
   reply:{jobId}                            the channel your answer comes back on
```

Both the queue and the answer channel are backed by Redis. The queue is a stream,
the answer channel is publish and subscribe, and the difference between them is
the figure above.

### When your machine moves between instances

It can happen, most often just after we deploy: your machine reconnects to a
different instance in the moment between the pointer being read and the job being
queued. The job lands in a queue nobody is listening to on your behalf.

The gateway does not sit on it. It reports immediately that the machine is not
there, and the sender looks the pointer up again and queues the job once more.

**Once, not repeatedly.** A machine flapping between instances fails the job
rather than having it bounce between queues, because a job that keeps moving is
worse than one that fails and can be asked again.

## The other heartbeat

A gateway instance writes to two registries, and they answer different questions.

<DeviceRegistries />

The distinction matters because the failures are not symmetrical. If the routing
registry stops, machines become unreachable and you notice at once. If the fleet
registry stops, every machine keeps working exactly as before while the platform
mis-counts how many gateway instances it has.

## How the platform knows your machine is there

It does not ask. There is no polling, no health check pointed at your machine,
and no field anywhere that says `online`.

**Absence is the signal.** The pointer is rewritten while the connection is held,
and it expires on its own if the connection stops. So:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **You open the lid** | The daemon dials out, proves itself, and the machine is available within seconds |
| **You close the lid** | The heartbeat stops, the pointer expires, and the machine is gone in about ninety seconds |
| **The network drops** | Identical to the above, with no special handling |
| **The machine crashes** | Also identical |

A crash, a closed lid and a pulled cable converge to the same state through the
same mechanism, which is why none of them needs to be detected.

<Callout>
  **Enrolled and offline is the normal state of a laptop.** Whether a machine is
  enrolled and whether it is connected are two independent things. A machine can
  be perfectly enrolled and switched off, and nothing about that needs repairing.
</Callout>

## What a deploy costs you

When a gateway instance is asked to stop, it does not simply drop the connections
it is holding. It tells each machine to reconnect, stops taking new work, and
gives them a few seconds to move before closing.

Your daemon treats that as an ordinary instruction rather than an error. It
reconnects, starting at about a second, and comes back on whichever instance
answers.

What that costs is one short interruption. What it can cost is the tail of a long
turn that was in flight, for the reason in the callout above.

## Revoking a machine

Revoking is a push rather than something the machine has to notice for itself.

Two things happen. The machine is marked revoked in the record the gateway reads
when a machine connects. And a message goes to the instance holding the
connection right now, which closes it in well under a second.

The daemon treats that particular refusal as permanent rather than as a network
problem, and stops trying for five minutes instead of reconnecting in a loop.

<Callout type="warn">
  **Revocation is checked when a connection is made, not continuously.** The live
  connection drops immediately, and a machine that tries to come back is refused.
  Treat the whole thing as taking effect on the order of a minute rather than
  instantly, and for a machine you have lost confidence in, do not make it your
  only step. If the machine is yours, stop the daemon on it as well.
</Callout>

## What the gateway deliberately does not do

**It does not decide who may use your machine.** It authenticates the machine and
nothing else. Which workspace may send work to which machine is resolved before a
job is ever queued, and no permission decision happens at the door.

**It holds no credentials.** Not yours, not ours. It verifies signatures against
a public key from a public record, which is what lets it be a box with nothing
worth stealing on it.

**It does not read what it carries.** The job it hands to your machine, the
output your machine streams back, and the result of a tool are all passed through
without being interpreted. That is what lets a new kind of work ship without
changing anything in the middle.

**It does not store anything.** No job history, no session, no transcript. A job
that fails mid flight leaves nothing behind on that side.
