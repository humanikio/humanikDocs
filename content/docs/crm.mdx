title: Contacts and conversations
description: One record per person, one thread across every channel.
---

The CRM is where every channel lands. A text message, an email, a phone call and
a booking all resolve to the same person and the same conversation.

That join is the point of it. An AI employee that answers a text and then follows
up by email is continuing one conversation, not starting a second one, and the
person on the other end experiences it that way too.

## One person, one thread

A contact is the stable identity for someone your workspace talks to. Their
conversation holds everything, in order, whatever channel it arrived on.

```
   Dana Reid
     ├─ 09:14  sms      "do you have anything Thursday?"
     ├─ 09:16  sms      "yes, 2pm or 4pm"
     ├─ 11:02  email    "confirming 2pm, here are the details"
     └─ 14:30  call     inbound, 4m12s
```

**The identity survives a channel change. The conversation does not fork.** This
is why a contact is not a phone number: one person can hold several numbers, and
the record has to outlive any of them.

## How someone becomes a contact

Most of the time this happens by itself. Someone texts a number you own, and a
contact exists before anyone has done anything.

What that resolution does depends on how they arrived, and the differences
matter:

| They arrive by | Matched on | Two at once |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Text or call | Their number | Safe. A call and a text landing together produce one contact |
| Email or a booking | Their address | Not guaranteed. Two simultaneous first touches can produce two records |
| CSV import | Their number, and repeats within the file | Skipped rather than duplicated |
| Creating one through the API | **Nothing** | Always creates |

<Callout type="warn">
  **Creating a contact does not check whether one already exists.** It makes a
  new record every time, and if you supply a phone number it takes ownership of
  that number, pointing it away from whoever held it before. Check first with
  [Find a contact by phone](/docs/api-reference/crm/contacts/findContactByPhone).
</Callout>

### Someone who texts and later emails becomes two contacts

This is deliberate, and worth understanding rather than working around.

A contact created from a text has a number and no address. When that same person
emails you, nothing matches them, so a second record appears.

The alternative is guessing that a number and an address belong to the same
human, and a wrong guess puts one person's conversation in front of another's.
The system will not do that.

<Callout type="warn">
  **There is no merge.** Once two records exist for one person, nothing joins
  them. Deciding which address or number is canonical, before people are in the
  system, is worth more here than it looks.
</Callout>

## Reaching people

Two outbound channels, both reaching a real person under your own identity.

**Text messages** go from a number allocated to that workspace. You can name
which one, and anything outside the workspace's own numbers is refused. Left
unspecified it uses the number that conversation is already on, or the
workspace's only number, or asks you to choose.

**Email** goes from an address your workspace holds. Who may send from which
address depends on the sender: a person with broad permission can use any address
in the account, and anyone else, including an AI employee, is limited to
addresses assigned to them specifically.

### What you write is not quite what is sent

Both channels support placeholders like `{{contact.first_name}}`, and they are
filled in **at the moment of sending**, against the person receiving it.

The practical consequence: you compose a template, and the finished message is
only visible afterwards, in the thread. Nobody sees the final wording before it
is delivered. Worth knowing before you rely on a proofread.

A placeholder naming a field you have defined but not filled resolves to nothing
rather than printing the raw token.

## Opting out

One flag per contact covers **every** channel. Marking someone opted out stops
texts, emails and calls together, and it is checked in four separate places
before anything leaves.

Someone replying STOP to a text sets it themselves, and that flows back to the
contact record.

<Callout type="warn">
  **There is no email unsubscribe.** STOP works on text messages. Nothing
  equivalent exists on email: no unsubscribe link is added, and no inbound
  handler watches for one. If you email marketing content, you are responsible
  for honouring opt-outs that arrive by reply.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warn">
  **The flag can be cleared by anything that can edit a contact.** It is an
  ordinary field, so the permission to update a contact is the permission to
  un-opt-out someone, including for an AI employee. If that matters to you, it
  is a reason to be careful with `contact:manage` rather than something the
  system prevents.
</Callout>

## What an AI employee can do here

This is the clearest example on the platform of an AI employee being an ordinary
member of staff rather than a special case.

An employee reaches the CRM **through the same endpoints a person's browser
calls**, carrying its own service account key, passing the same permission
checks. There is no agent-only path and no elevated access. A denial arrives as
a `403`, exactly as it would for a person.

Its key is bound to one workspace, and that binding comes from the credential
rather than from anything the model says, so an employee cannot aim a change at a
workspace it is not in even if it tried.

By default an employee can read contacts, tag them, create and update them, send
texts, send email **only from its own assigned addresses**, and manage deals.

<Callout type="warn">
  **Nothing asks you before a message is sent.** There is no approval step, no
  queue, and no confirmation on either channel. An employee that decides to send
  a text sends it, to a real person, immediately.

  The control you have is permissions, not review. If an employee should not be
  messaging people, do not grant it `phone:send` or the email actions. Withhold
  the capability rather than expecting to be asked.
</Callout>

That is the honest shape of it today. Instructions in a protocol are guidance,
and guidance is not a gate. See
[Skills and protocols](/docs/skills-and-protocols) for the difference.

## Notes, tags and custom fields

**Notes** are internal. They are attached to a contact, timestamped and
attributed, and the contact never sees them.

**Tags** are workspace-wide labels. The registry is shared, so a tag created for
one contact is available for all of them.

**Custom fields** are yours to define, typed, and available as placeholders in
messages.

## Deals

A **pipeline** is an ordered list of stages. An **opportunity** is a deal sitting
in one stage of one pipeline, optionally linked to a contact.

Two things to know before you build on them:

**The contact link is loose.** A deal stores a contact's ID and a copy of their
name, and neither is checked. The name is a snapshot that never refreshes, and
deleting a contact leaves their deals behind pointing at nothing.

**Deleting a pipeline deletes its deals.** There is no soft delete and no restore
window. Removing a stage from a pipeline, on the other hand, leaves deals sitting
in a stage that no longer exists.

<Callout>
  **Nothing fires when a deal is won.** Deals are not a trigger source for
  automations. If you want something to happen at a stage change, drive it from
  the change itself rather than expecting an event.
</Callout>

## What this is not

It is contacts, conversations, notes, tags, custom fields and a deal board.

There is **no reporting or forecasting**, no email campaign tooling, and no
metering on messaging: nothing counts or caps what is sent, so a
[spending limit](/docs/spend) does not reach it.

Unread counts are **shared across the workspace**, not per person. When one
person opens a conversation it is read for everybody.
