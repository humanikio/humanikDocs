---
title: Where offices run
description: The machines behind your AI employees, how they sleep and resume, and what they cost while awake.
---

Every office runs on a machine. Understanding when that machine exists, and when
it does not, is most of what you need to reason about performance and cost.

The arrangement is unusual and it is worth naming before the detail. **The unit
of compute here is an employee, not a request and not a replica.** A machine
belongs to one employee, holds that employee's filesystem and credentials, and
scales on its own. A workspace with forty employees is forty independent
machines, each sleeping and waking on its own schedule, and none of them is
holding a slot for any of the others.

## An office is a service

An office is not a process inside a shared system. It is its own machine, with
its own environment, its own secrets, and its own view of your data.

That is why an office can be resized, given an idle timeout, inspected while
running, and read for logs. It is also why two offices cannot see each other's
credentials: they are not in the same place.

It is also why the size of a workspace is not a capacity question you have to
plan. Nothing is shared between employees, so adding one does not take anything
away from another, and an employee that is busy does not slow down an employee
that is idle.

## Sleeping is the normal state

An office that is not working does not exist as a running machine, and **an
employee that is not working costs nothing to run.**

```
   work arrives ──▶ machine starts ──▶ works ──▶ idle ──▶ sleeps
                         ▲                                  │
                         └──────────  work arrives  ────────┘
```

Nothing is lost when it sleeps. Its configuration, its data and its
conversations are not on the machine.

### Waking is a restore, not a fresh start

This is the part that makes sleeping worth doing, and it is more than the
machine coming back.

An office's filesystem is captured and stored, and its configuration and
conversation live off the machine entirely. So waking is a restore: the
filesystem comes back, the configuration changes made since the snapshot are
applied on top, and the conversation is rehydrated before the agent starts.

The practical consequence is the one customers notice. **An employee that wakes
on Thursday still has what it did on Monday.** A repository it cloned is still
cloned. A file it left itself is still there. A conversation that stopped
mid-thread continues rather than beginning again. The machine not existing for a
week is not an event the employee experiences.

### The first request after sleeping waits

Waking is not instant. A request arriving at a sleeping office does not fail, it
waits while the machine starts, and then it is served.

How long depends on the size of the machine and what it has to load. For
interactive work this is the one thing worth designing around: if somebody is
waiting on the other end, a first message after a quiet period feels slower than
the ones after it.

If that matters, keep the office awake rather than trying to make waking faster.

<Callout>
  **Sleeping is a cost decision, not a correctness one.** An office that sleeps
  and one that never does behave identically. The difference is what you pay and
  how the first message feels.
</Callout>

## The two levers on cost

Machine time is billed while an office is awake, and unlike model spend it
**cannot be capped**. A [spending limit](/docs/spend) does not reach it.

So the controls are structural rather than a ceiling:

| Lever | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| **Size** | A smaller machine costs less for every second it is awake |
| **Idle timeout** | A shorter one means fewer seconds awake |

Because each employee carries its own size and its own timeout, these are per
employee decisions rather than one setting for the account. An office that
handles inbound calls during business hours and one that runs a nightly
reconciliation want opposite answers, and they can have them.

An office left running with a long timeout is the usual explanation for a
compute bill nobody expected. Nothing will stop it, and nothing will warn you.

You can also stop one directly with
[Shut down an office](/docs/api-reference/offices/machine/shutdownOfficeMachine),
which is graceful: work in progress finishes rather than being cut off.

## Configuration is read at startup

An office reads its settings once, when it starts, and holds them for as long as
that machine lives.

| Changing | Applies |
| --- | --- |
| Skills | Immediately |
| Model provider, framework, secrets, integrations, protocols | At the next restart |

<Callout type="warn">
  **Restarting means replacing the machine, not rebooting it.**
  [Restart an office](/docs/api-reference/offices/machine/restartOfficeMachine)
  builds a fresh machine with the current settings, brings it up, checks it, and
  only then stops the old one. Rebooting the existing machine would keep what it
  was built with, which is exactly what you are trying to change.
</Callout>

There is no gap in service while that happens.

You do not have to remember which changes are waiting. When something has been
changed and not yet applied, the console says so and names the specific changes,
with a control to apply them.

## What else runs here

Offices are the common case, but they are not the only thing. Anything running
for your account appears in the same place, which is why the cost breakdown
separates what it can identify as an office from what it cannot.

Use the [Humanik Cloud API](/docs/api-reference/cloud) when you want a view
across everything, and the office's own endpoints when you are working with one
employee. The office route is narrower, and it can be granted to someone
responsible for a single employee without giving them the whole account.

<Callout type="warn">
  **Do not create or delete an office as a service.** Creating an office through
  [Create an office](/docs/api-reference/offices/offices/createOffice) sets up its
  identity, its vault and its data access alongside the machine. A bare service
  has none of that, and deleting the service alone leaves an office whose machine
  no longer exists.
</Callout>
