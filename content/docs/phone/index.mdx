---
title: Phone numbers
description: Buying a number, giving it to an employee, and what changes the moment you do.
---

A phone number is something your organization owns, in the same sense as a
workspace or a domain. You buy one inside the product, hand it down to the people
and employees who should use it, and configure it per number.

What makes a number different from every other asset is what happens after you
hand it over. A domain gives an employee an address to send from. A number gives
an employee a line that rings, with a person on the other end who is waiting for
an answer right now. Almost everything on these pages follows from that one
difference.

## Nobody has to set up telephony

There is no account to open with a phone company, no credentials to paste in, and
no connector to configure. You search available numbers from inside your
organization, buy one, and it exists.

<Callout>
  The other side of that is worth knowing before you buy: **you cannot bring a
  number or an account you already have.** Numbers come from inside the product,
  and they are the only numbers your employees can use.
</Callout>

## Who can hold a number

<AllocationChain />

Three levels, and each one is a different decision made by a different person.

**Your organization owns it.** Buying a number spends money every month from the
moment it is bought, so it sits with someone who administers the organization
rather than with an individual team.

**One workspace holds it.** A number belongs to exactly one workspace and cannot
be shared between two. Pointing it somewhere else means taking it back from the
first workspace before giving it to the second.

**Any number of employees can use it, and exactly one answers it.** The employee
holding the number as **primary** is the one that receives incoming calls and
texts. The others can call and text out from it, which is what lets a team of
employees present one business line.

<Callout type="warn">
  **Promoting a different employee to primary moves inbound, it does not add a
  second recipient.** There is no ringing of two employees, and no queue. If two
  employees should each answer their own calls, they need a number each.
</Callout>

### Or give it to Nova instead of an employee

A number does not have to answer as the business. It can be assigned to **Nova**,
and then texting it reaches the assistant belonging to whoever sent the message,
with that person's own permissions and budget.

The difference is who the number speaks for. An employee holding a number answers
your customers as the business, and anyone can text it. A number assigned to Nova
answers only the people in that workspace who have added and confirmed their own
mobile number, and it answers each of them as themselves.

<Callout type="warn">
  **A number goes to Nova or to an employee, never to both.** Switching removes
  the previous assignment. The console names what it is about to remove before
  you confirm.
</Callout>

Nova numbers carry text messages only. They do not answer calls. Everything about
setting one up is on [Texting Nova](/docs/nova/texting).

### Giving a number to an employee also gives it to the workspace

You do not have to do both. Allocating a number to an employee records the
workspace allocation at the same time, so the two cannot drift apart, and an
administrator who only ever uses the employee picker still ends up with correct
records underneath.

## What happens the moment you allocate

Three things, none of which you do by hand.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Incoming calls start arriving** | The number is wired to us when it is allocated, not when someone remembers to configure it. Doing it again is harmless. |
| **Text messaging turns on** | Inbound texts reach the primary employee, and it replies. Auto reply is on by default, per number, and you can turn it off. See [Text messaging](/docs/phone/texting). |
| **Voicemail turns on** | Anything the employee cannot take becomes a message rather than a dead line. See [Voicemail](/docs/phone/voicemail). |

If you want a number that does not text, or does not take messages, turn those
off after allocating rather than expecting them to be off to begin with.

## Taking a number back

Two different actions, and only one of them is reversible.

**Deallocating** removes the number from an employee or a workspace. The number
stays yours and can be given to somebody else. This is the one you want almost
every time.

**Releasing** gives the number up entirely. It stops being yours, it stops being
billed, and it returns to the public pool where it may be given to someone else.

<Callout type="warn">
  **Releasing cannot be undone, and nothing checks what the number was doing
  first.** There is no warning about calls in progress, employees holding it, or
  conversations that reference it. Deallocate when you mean "stop using this
  here". Release only when you mean "we no longer want this number at all".
</Callout>

An employee that has just had a number taken away may keep using it for outgoing
messages until its machine next restarts. Removing the capability from the
employee is the reliable control, not removing the number.

## What an employee can do with one

| | Needs |
| --- | --- |
| Answer an incoming call | Holding the number as primary |
| Speak on that call, look things up, and act during it | Its ordinary permissions |
| Place a call | A number assigned to it |
| Send a text | [`phone:send`](/docs/access-control/actions) at the workspace |
| Buy, allocate or configure a number | **Nothing. An employee cannot do this at all** |

The last row is the shape of the whole system. Acquiring a capability is a human
action, exercising it is a machine action, and the split is enforced rather than
advised: the entire number management surface refuses a service account key, so
an employee cannot buy itself a line however it is asked.

<Callout>
  The built in [`officeAgent`](/docs/access-control/roles) role holds
  `phone:send` and not `phone:call`. An employee with that role texts from its
  number but cannot mint a dialing credential for a browser. That distinction is
  doing real work rather than decorating the list.
</Callout>

## What this does not do

**Nothing meters calls or messages.** No budget, no cap, and no usage record
reaches [spending limits](/docs/spend). A number costs what it costs and the
traffic on it is unbounded.

**Nothing asks you before an employee calls or texts someone.** There is no
approval step and no review queue. The control is the permission and the
allocation: an employee with no number cannot place a call, and an employee
without `phone:send` cannot text.

**An incoming call or text does not start a workflow.** They land in the
conversation and reach the employee. They are not a trigger.

**Routing is per number, not per organization.** There are no business hours, no
queues, and no overflow groups.
