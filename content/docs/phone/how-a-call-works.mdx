---
title: The path of a call
description: What happens between someone dialing your number and hearing your employee answer.
---

Most software that answers phones is a thin layer over somebody else's calling
product. The call is held elsewhere, a transcript arrives afterwards, and the
software reads it.

This is not that. The call arrives on a number you own, we decide who answers it,
we hold the live session on our own servers for the length of the call, and the
employee that speaks is the same employee that reads your inbox and updates your
records. There is no handoff to a voice product and no transcript to catch up on
later.

This page follows one call from the first ring to the last word.

## The whole path

<CallPath />

### 1. The call arrives with almost nothing

A caller dials. What reaches us is the number they dialled, and that is the only
thing we have to work with. So the first job is resolution: turning a number into
one workspace, and then into the one employee set to receive on it.

That lookup is the reason a number can only belong to one workspace. There is no
ambiguity to resolve at the moment a person is waiting.

### 2. Waking the employee starts before we answer

An employee that is not working does not exist as a running machine. See
[Where offices run](/docs/cloud). A caller will not wait for one to start, so
waking it and answering the call happen at the same time rather than one after
the other.

<Callout type="warn">
  **A call to a sleeping employee takes a message.** Rather than hold the line
  through a start up, the caller rings for about eight seconds and reaches
  voicemail, while the machine wakes in the background. The next call connects
  normally.

  If calls arriving on a quiet line matter to you, that is a reason to keep the
  employee awake rather than to expect waking to get faster. Keeping it awake is
  a setting, and it costs money while nothing is happening.
</Callout>

### 3. The session is ours, and it is held open

Once we answer, one connection stays open for the whole call. Every sentence the
caller says arrives on it, and every word of the answer leaves on it.

The address of that session is signed by us and valid only briefly, so a session
cannot be resumed, replayed, or opened by anything that did not get the address
from us. If we cannot sign it, the call does not connect. Failing closed is the
deliberate choice.

### 4. Each sentence is a round trip to your employee

A turn is not a message queued for later. The caller finishes a sentence, that
sentence goes to the employee's own machine, and the reply comes back word by
word while the caller is still on the line.

The employee is not a voice bot with a copy of your data. It is the machine that
holds your credentials, your tools, your files and your records, so during the
call it can look something up, write it down, send a text, or check a calendar,
and then say the answer.

### 5. The answer starts speaking before it is finished

Words leave for the phone network the moment they exist. The caller hears the
start of a reply while the rest of it is still being written, which is what makes
the conversation feel like a conversation rather than a series of pauses.

## Hearing and speaking

<VoicePipeline />

Two of those three steps happen in the media path, where the audio already is.
Doing them there rather than shipping audio to ourselves and back is most of the
reason a reply starts quickly.

**Hearing.** The caller's speech is transcribed live and we receive finished
sentences, not audio. Half finished guesses arrive too, and we ignore them: a
turn begins only when a sentence is complete.

**Speaking.** Text is turned into speech by a voice provider as it arrives. We
send pieces, not paragraphs.

<Callout type="warn">
  **Live transcription is English today.** Speech in another language is not
  transcribed, which means the employee does not hear it and it does not appear
  in the record. A caller who switches to English is heard again from that point.
</Callout>

### Two brains, one mouth

Inside a turn there are two different jobs with two different speeds, and the
system treats them as two different things.

A **fast model writes the words the caller hears**. It owns the opening of every
turn: the greeting, the acknowledgement, the small talk, anything already
established on this call.

Anything it could not know is handed to the **employee**, which does the actual
work: reads the record, checks the calendar, sends the message. It then speaks
its own answer directly to the caller.

That last part is the design decision worth naming. There is no second model
taking the employee's answer and saying it again in its own words. The employee
writes for the ear and speaks for itself, so an answer cannot be finished and
then lost on the way to the caller.

<Callout>
  **The line is not silent while work happens.** The employee narrates as it
  goes, so a caller who asks something that takes real work hears what is being
  done rather than a gap. Anything found during the call is also remembered for
  the rest of it, so asking again later is answered immediately instead of
  looked up twice.
</Callout>

### When somebody talks over the answer

A caller interrupting the employee mid sentence cancels the reply and takes the
new sentence, which is what an interruption should do.

A caller who speaks in a gap, before anything has been said, is treated
differently: the turn already under way is allowed to speak, and the extra
sentence is recorded rather than answered. Answering both would mean talking over
ourselves.

## The call is one conversation, not a series of requests

One identifier is created when the call starts and follows it everywhere: the
wake up, the live session, the employee's memory of this call, and the record
written into the CRM.

That is what makes the employee remember the third sentence when the caller
refers back to the first. It is one session, kept for the call, rather than a
fresh model call each time with the previous text pasted in front of it.

## How the call stays in the system

<Callout>
  **Every turn is written as it happens, not at the end.** A call that drops
  halfway, or is cut off by a network problem, still leaves everything said up to
  that point in the record.
</Callout>

The call lands in the [contact's one conversation](/docs/crm), next to their
texts and their email. There is no separate call log to go and read.

What is on the record:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Who it was** | The caller is resolved to a contact, and a new one is created if they are not known yet |
| **What was said** | Each turn, labelled as the caller or the employee |
| **What happened** | Direction, status, and how long it lasted |
| **The audio** | Only if recording is turned on for that number. See [Recording and transcripts](/docs/phone/recording-and-transcripts) |

Calls placed by your people from the browser land in the same place as calls
answered by an employee. One person, one thread, whatever channel it came in on.

## When something goes wrong

**If the employee produces nothing, the call stays silent.** It does not say
"sorry, I did not catch that", because that is not what happened: the caller was
heard, and our processing failed. A misleading apology is worse than a pause the
caller can speak into.

**One slow turn costs one turn.** A turn that hangs is given a hard limit, then
closed, so the next thing the caller says is heard normally. The first turn of a
call is allowed longer than the rest, because it is the one that pays for any
remaining wake up.
