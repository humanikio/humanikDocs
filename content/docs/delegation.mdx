---
title: Delegation
description: How an AI employee finds the right colleague and hands work over.
---

Ask an AI employee *"who handles billing questions?"* and it needs a real answer.

Without one, an agent does what agents do with a gap: it invents a colleague, or
it tells the person it cannot help. Neither is what someone in an organisation
does, because a person knows who sits next to them.

## Two questions, kept separate

Handing work to the right colleague needs two different facts, and they come from
different places.

| Question | Comes from |
| --- | --- |
| **Can they do this?** | What that employee is equipped with: its instructions, its scheduled work, its connected tools |
| **Are they senior enough?** | What that employee is permitted to do |

That split is the whole design. An employee looking for someone to *do* a job
reads the first. An employee looking for someone to *approve* a job reads the
second.

## People and AI employees are on the same chart

Everyone in a workspace appears in one list: your colleagues, your AI employees,
and any other machine identity you have created.

They are ordered on the same scale, which means **an AI employee escalating to a
person and escalating to another AI employee are the same operation.** There is
no separate path for handing something to a human.

That ordering is derived from permissions rather than assigned by hand. Someone
who can approve spending sits above someone who can read a contact, because that
is what their access says.

<Callout>
  **This is not a performance score.** The ordering exists so an employee can
  tell an escalation from a hand-off. It says who may decide what, not who is
  doing well.
</Callout>

## Nothing here changes anyone's access

Worth being unambiguous about, because a system where a model writes into
anything permission-shaped deserves the question.

**Permissions flow in. Nothing flows back out.** The ordering is computed from
access and validated against it, and changing the ordering changes no one's
access. An AI employee cannot promote anyone, including itself, and neither can
the model that helps maintain the chart.

An AI employee's own view of the chart is **read-only**. It can read who does
what; it cannot edit the list, reorder it, or add anyone to it. Editing lives
with people.

### Where a model does help, and how that is bounded

Permissions alone do not capture everything. Someone who administers nothing may
still do the most valuable work, and a chart derived purely from access would put
them at the bottom.

So a model reads what each employee is actually equipped with and **proposes** a
description and an adjustment. Proposals are not applied. An administrator
resolves each one individually, and there is no accept-all.

<Callout type="warn">
  **Descriptions are written by a model and approved once.** They are a signal
  for routing, not a verified account of what an employee does. They are not
  rechecked as an employee changes, so an employee that gained a capability last
  month may still be described without it.
</Callout>

## Staying honest as things change

When someone's access changes, their entry is flagged as out of date rather than
silently left wrong. An employee reading the chart can see that a particular
entry is stale and weigh it accordingly.

Detecting drift is not the same as fixing it. A stale entry stays stale until
someone recomputes it, and nothing chases that on your behalf.

## Then it routes

Finding the right colleague is only half of it. The employee reads the
descriptions, picks someone who fits or someone senior enough to decide, and
sends the work to them in a conversation.

```
   "this needs a refund over $500"
              │
              ▼
     who can approve that?          ← the chart answers
              │
              ▼
     message that colleague         ← the conversation carries it
```

**The chart answers who. The conversation carries the work.** They are the same
organisation seen twice: the structure, and the traffic moving along it.

## What it will not do

**It will not invent a colleague.** The list is the workspace's real membership,
which is precisely what stops an employee routing work to somebody who does not
exist. This was the original problem, and the fix is that there is now something
true to read.

**It will not always pick correctly.** Descriptions and ordering are signals, not
guarantees. An employee can misjudge fit exactly as a person can.

**It is not a reporting line.** One structure for finding people, not a
management relationship. Nothing reports to anything.
